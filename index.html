0. Before using the tool, what type of pressure injury do you think this wound is?

1. Is the skin/ mucosal membrane normal?

1a. Investigate the wound's etiology.
Ask the patient or family and check the patient's chart, how did the patient obtain THE wound?

1b. BOTH incontinent and immobile patients, pressure injuries and IAD may co-exist—even when wounds are close together. What does the wound look like?

Assess and document each wound separately if necessary and carefully noting the anatomical location.

2. Location alone does not determine pressure injury.
Etiology (pressure, shear, or device-related compression) is the key factor.
Is this area usually pressed against the bed, chair, or a medical device — or has it been sliding/rubbing (shear)?

In incontinent and immobile patients, pressure injuries and IAD may co-exist—even when wounds are close together. Assess and document each wound separately, carefully noting the anatomical location.

Common shear risk sites: sacrum/coccyx, ischial tuberosities, heels, posterior thighs, elbows — especially if sliding down in bed/chair or being boosted without lift.

2a. If pressure/shear/device pressure is not identified, is another cause more likely?

Examples: trauma/fall, surgical wound, vascular ulcer (PAD/PVD), diabetic ulcer, infection/cellulitis, dermatitis/rash.

3. Is it an open wound?

4. Do you see muscle/ bone/ tendon structure?

5.Do you see subcutaneous fat (yellow/tan lobulated adipose)?

5a. Is slough or eschar present anywhere in the wound bed?

5b. Is the wound depth obscured by slough or eschar? (If you cannot clearly see the deepest tissue layer, select Yes.)

6. Do you see any blister? Palpate skin, do you feel boggy/ mushy?

7. What color is the blister?

8. Is the skin blanchable?
i.e. does the skin temporarily turns white or pale when pressure is applied?

9. If the skin does not turn white when you apply pressure, what color is the skin?

10.Is the wound related to moisture?

In incontinent and immobile patients, pressure injuries and IAD may co-exist—even when wounds are close together. Assess and document each wound separately, carefully noting the anatomical location.

11. Is the wound located at the perigenital/perineal area?

In incontinent and immobile patients, pressure injuries and IAD may co-exist—even when wounds are close together. Assess and document each wound separately, carefully noting the anatomical location.

12. Is the wound caused by shear force? Shear example: sliding down in bed/chair.

13. Is the non pressure injury wound open?

14. Is the IAD showing full-thickness loss?
